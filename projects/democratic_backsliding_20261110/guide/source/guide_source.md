## 1. The whole talk in one minute

Your talk tells one story in seven steps.

1. **Democracies today rarely end by force.** In the past, soldiers often took power in a coup. Today, most democracies that break down are ended by leaders who were elected. *(slides 1 and 2)*
2. **This is called democratic backsliding.** An elected government makes its own democracy weaker, step by step. Each step is small and legal. *(slide 2)*
3. **It is not a coup and not a fascist takeover.** It is slower and quieter, and the country still looks like a democracy. *(slide 3)*
4. **It follows a path.** First a divided society, then a big election win, then many small legal steps. *(slide 4)*
5. **The steps are aimed at three “referees”.** These are parliament, the courts and the election authorities. Their job is to make the government follow the rules. Instead, the government takes control of them. This is called institutional capture. *(slides 5 to 7)*
6. **It is happening in many countries.** 41 percent of the world’s people live in countries that are becoming less democratic. *(slide 8)*
7. **It can be voted out, but repair is hard.** Hungary voted its government out in April 2026. Now the new government has to repair the damage without using bad methods itself. *(slide 9)*

> **If you remember only one sentence**
> Today’s democracies are not ended by force. They are taken apart slowly and legally, by governments that won elections.

#### What the title means
- **Democratic backsliding:** a democracy slides back. It slowly becomes less democratic.
- **Institutional capture:** the government “captures” the institutions that should control it. They still exist, but now they work for the government.

### Slide 1 · Cover
@headline How elected governments take control of parliaments, courts and election authorities, one legal step at a time

#### What you say
{{script 1 | First I explain what backsliding is}}
@stress “not with bullets but with laws”

#### What it means
The quote gives your whole talk in one line. *Bullets* stands for violence: soldiers and guns. *Laws* stands for legal changes: new laws, new rules, new judges.

So the quote says: today’s autocrats do not fight their way to power. They win an election. Then they change the laws until nobody can stop them.

#### Good to know
- **Kim Lane Scheppele** is a professor at Princeton University in the United States. She studies how leaders use the law to get more power.
- **The quote** comes from her article “Autocratic Legalism” (2018). Autocratic legalism means: using the law to remove the limits on your own power.
- **The photo** shows the Hungarian Parliament in Budapest at night. Hungary is the main example in your talk.

### Slide 2 · What it is
@headline Democracies today are rarely ended by force. They are taken apart slowly, from the inside.

#### What you say
{{script 2 | First, it happens step by step | How common is it?}}
@stress four out of five

#### What it means
This slide gives the definition. Backsliding means: a democracy is made weaker step by step, and its own elected government does this. The small numbers 1, 2 and 3 in the definition lead to the three points below it.

1. **Step by step.** There is no single big event. There are many small actions, sometimes with long pauses between them.
2. **Aimed at the limits on power.** The government does not close the courts or stop elections. It makes them weaker, so that they can no longer stop the government.
3. **Done by an elected government.** The people who weaken democracy came to power in a normal election.

The right side shows how common this is. Milan Svolik, a professor at Yale University, counted 197 cases of democratic decline between 1973 and 2018. Elected leaders were behind 88 of them. Military coups were behind 46. And since the 2000s, four out of five democracies that broke down were ended by their own elected leaders.

#### Good to know
- **The exact words of Haggard and Kaufman** (page 27): “the incremental erosion of institutions, rules, and norms that results from the actions of duly elected governments”. *Incremental* means step by step. *Erosion* means slow damage, like water that slowly wears away a stone. *Norms* are unwritten rules of fair behaviour. *Duly elected* means elected in the correct way.
- **The exact words of Waldner and Lust** (page 95): “a discontinuous series of incremental actions, not a one-time coup de grâce”. *Discontinuous* means with pauses. *Coup de grâce* is French and means the final blow that kills.
- **Svolik’s numbers** use the ratings of Freedom House. This American organization rates every year how free each country is. A “case of democratic decline” is a year in which it moved a country down to a lower group. The cases that are not in the two bars had other causes, for example civil war.

#### If someone asks
**“Is every bad law backsliding?”**
No. Waldner and Lust warn about this. A fight about one rule is normal politics. It is backsliding when a government changes things on purpose, so that elections become less fair, fewer people can take part, or nobody can control the government any more.

### Slide 3 · What it is not
@headline Backsliding is not a coup or a fascist takeover: it is slower, quieter and still looks democratic.

#### What you say
{{script 3 | The fascist takeover, in Italy | Backsliding is done by a government}}
@stress Being legal is not the difference. Speed, terror and the result are.

#### What it means
This slide compares three ways in which a democracy can end. The table asks the same five questions about each of them: Who acts? Is it legal? How fast? Is it violent? What is the result?

- **A coup** is a sudden and illegal takeover. The army, or a small group inside the state, takes power by force. It is over in hours or days. After a coup there are no elections.
- **A fascist takeover** is the way Mussolini came to power in Italy in 1922, and Hitler in Germany in 1933. It was legal on paper, but it was fast and violent. It ended in an open dictatorship.
- **Backsliding** is done by a government that won an election. Every step is legal. It takes years. There is little violence. And the country still looks like a democracy: it still has elections, courts and a parliament.

The last lines of your script are the most important ones. Hitler’s way to power was also legal on paper. So being legal is not what makes backsliding different. The differences are speed (weeks, not years), terror, and the result (an open dictatorship, not a country that still looks democratic).

#### Good to know
- **Italy 1922.** Mussolini’s fascist party had its own armed fighters. In October 1922 they marched on Rome. The king did not stop them. He asked Mussolini to lead the government.
- **Germany 1933.** On 30 January the president made Hitler head of the government. On 27 February the parliament building, the Reichstag, burned. On 23 March parliament passed the Enabling Act. This law let Hitler’s government make laws without parliament. That was 24 days after the fire.
- **The 107 MPs.** The Nazis stopped all 81 Communist MPs and 26 Social Democratic MPs from taking their seats. Many of them were under arrest. 81 plus 26 is 107.
- **“Invited in by those already in power”** is an idea of the historian Robert Paxton. Fascists did not take power alone. Conservative leaders opened the door for them.
- **A coup “by the leader”** means that an elected leader suddenly closes parliament or the courts by force. This is also called a self-coup.

#### If someone asks
**“Was Hitler not elected too?”**
Only partly. His party was the biggest party, but it never won a majority in a free election. In November 1932 it got 33 percent of the votes. Then the president appointed Hitler. In backsliding, the leader really wins the election, and no terror is needed.

### Slide 4 · How it works
@headline The path: a divided society, a big election win, then capture in small, legal steps.

#### What you say
{{script 4 | Step two is a big election win. | Step three is capture.}}
@stress 34 percent of the votes, 66 percent of the seats

#### What it means
This slide shows the path in three steps. Haggard and Kaufman found this path in the sixteen countries that they studied.

1. **A divided society.** People split into two camps that see each other as enemies. This is called polarization. When politics feels like a fight between enemies, many voters forgive their own side for breaking the rules. Polarization went up in 12 of the 16 countries.
2. **A big election win.** The leader’s party also wins parliament. Parliament should control the government. But when one party runs both, parliament stops saying no. The example is Turkey in 2002: 34 percent of the votes became 66 percent of the seats.
3. **Capture in small steps.** The government takes control of the institutions, one small and legal step at a time. One thin slice of a salami is not much. But slice by slice, the whole salami is gone. In 12 of the 16 countries the courts were attacked.

The sentence in the box at the bottom gives Wolin’s idea in simple words: a government with a majority can change everything in a legal way. It needs no revolution.

At the bottom right you see the three targets: parliament, the courts and the election authorities. Your talk calls them *referees*. In football, the referee makes both teams follow the rules. These three institutions do the same job in a state. The next three slides take them one by one.

#### Good to know
- **Why 34 percent became 66 percent in Turkey.** A party needed 10 percent of all votes to enter parliament. Only two parties reached this. The votes for all other parties were lost, so the two parties shared almost all the seats.
- **The sixteen countries**, as listed in the authors’ book *Backsliding* (2021): Bolivia, Brazil, the Dominican Republic, Ecuador, Greece, Hungary, North Macedonia, Nicaragua, Poland, Russia, Serbia, Turkey, Ukraine, the United States, Venezuela and Zambia.
- **Salami.** Haggard and Kaufman’s own words are “salami-slicing tactics”.
- **Wolin’s exact words** (page 245): the majority holds “the legal means of achieving revolutionary ends without having to resort to revolutionary means”. *Ends* means goals. *To resort to* means to use.

#### If someone asks
**“Why do voters accept this?”**
Milan Svolik’s answer is polarization. In a divided country, many voters care more about their own side winning than about the rules of democracy. So they accept a leader who bends the rules, as long as it is their leader.

### Slide 5 · Referee 1 of 3: Parliament
@headline A loyal parliament makes every later step legal, and even gives away its own power.

#### What you say
{{script 5 | First, it fires the other referees. | Second, it removes the limits | Third, it gives its own power | Haggard and Kaufman see the same pattern}}
@stress 137 of 199 MPs voted to give away their own power

#### What it means
Parliament is the first referee, because parliament makes the laws. If parliament is loyal to the leader, every later step can be made legal. The slide shows three things that a loyal parliament does.

1. **It fires the other referees.** In El Salvador, a new parliament met for the first time on 1 May 2021. The party of President Nayib Bukele had a large majority. On that same day, parliament fired five top judges and the chief prosecutor, and it put new people in their place.
2. **It removes the limits on the leader.** In 2025, El Salvador’s parliament removed the rule that limits how many terms a president can serve. Now the president can be elected again and again.
3. **It gives its own power to the leader.** On 30 March 2020, at the start of the COVID-19 pandemic, Hungary’s parliament let Prime Minister Viktor Orbán rule by decree. He could make rules alone, without a vote in parliament, and there was no end date. 137 of 199 MPs voted yes.

The idea behind the slide: nobody forced these parliaments. They did it themselves.

#### Good to know
- **The exact words of Haggard and Kaufman** (pages 34 and 35): parliaments were “frequently complicit in weakening their own powers”. *Complicit* means helping to do something wrong.
- **Separation of powers.** Haggard and Kaufman call this step the “collapse of the separation of powers”. Separation of powers means that parliament, government and courts are separate, and each one controls the others.
- **What happened next in Hungary.** The rule by decree of March 2020 ended in June 2020. Later that year the government declared a new emergency. Emergency rule then continued in different forms, until the new government let it end in May 2026.
- **The photo** shows the debating chamber of the Hungarian Parliament.

#### If someone asks
**“Why do MPs give away their own power?”**
Because they belong to the leader’s party, and their careers depend on the leader. So loyalty to the party is stronger than loyalty to parliament.

**“Many countries had emergency rules in the pandemic. What was different in Hungary?”**
The Hungarian law had no end date. So the leader could rule alone for as long as he wanted, until parliament took the power back.

### Slide 6 · Referee 2 of 3: The courts
@headline Captured courts stop being neutral: they protect the government and punish its opponents.

#### What you say
{{script 6 | One way to capture a court | Another way is to push | The third way is to control | Does it work?}}
@stress 0 of 10 before, 10 of 13 after

#### What it means
Courts decide whether the government has broken the law. So a government that wants no limits must control the courts. The slide shows three ways to do this.

1. **Add seats and fill them with loyal judges.** Hungary made its top court bigger in 2011, from 11 to 15 judges. Venezuela did the same in 2004, from 20 to 32 judges. The governing side chose the new judges, so it got a majority in the court.
2. **Push the current judges out.** After a failed coup in July 2016, Turkey fired more than 4,000 judges and prosecutors.
3. **Control who becomes a judge.** In Poland, a council proposes new judges. Before 2018, judges chose most members of this council. Since 2018, parliament has chosen them. About 2,700 judges were appointed or promoted in this way. That is about one third of all Polish judges.

Does it work? The big number answers this. The Hungarian Helsinki Committee, a human rights group in Budapest, studied 23 important cases of Hungary’s Constitutional Court. Before the government’s judges were the majority, 0 of 10 decisions went the government’s way. After that, 10 of 13 did.

The quote at the bottom says that capturing the referees gives a government “more than a shield”. It is also “a powerful weapon”. A shield protects: the court stops cases against the government. A weapon attacks: the court is used against opponents.

#### Good to know
- **Where the word “referees” comes from.** It comes from the book *How Democracies Die* (2018) by Steven Levitsky and Daniel Ziblatt, two professors at Harvard University. They compare politics to a football match. A leader who wants to cheat first captures the referees.
- **Court packing** is the usual name for adding seats to a court in order to get a majority.
- **How Hungary did it.** In 2010 the rules were changed, so that the governing majority could choose new judges without the opposition. In 2011 the court grew from 11 to 15 judges. In the end, the governing parties alone had chosen 11 of the 15 judges.
- **Another trick from Hungary.** In 2011 Hungary lowered the age at which judges must stop working, from 70 to 62. This pushed out older judges. In 2012 the top court of the EU said that this was against EU law.
- **Turkey 2016.** On 15 July 2016, a part of the army tried to take power and failed. After that, the government fired or arrested very many people, also judges.
- **The photo** shows the Constitutional Court of Hungary in Budapest.

#### If someone asks
**“Every government chooses judges in some way. What is different here?”**
In a healthy system, judges are chosen by fixed rules, and the government cannot remove them when it dislikes a decision. In a capture, the government changes the rules so that it quickly gets a loyal majority.

### Slide 7 · Referee 3 of 3: Election authorities
@headline Captured election authorities can decide the result before election day, or after it.

#### What you say
{{script 7 | One move is to change the map | Another is to decide who is allowed | The third move comes after election day | Venezuela shows where this ends.}}
@stress 51.2 percent, with no detailed results

#### What it means
Election authorities are the offices that organize elections. They decide who may run, they count the votes and they announce the result. When the government controls them, elections still take place. But the result can be decided before election day, or after it.

1. **Before: change the map and the rules.** Hungary has 106 election districts, and each district elects one MP. Whoever draws the borders of the districts can help one party. In 2014, the governing parties got about 45 percent of the votes, but two thirds of all seats. They won 96 of the 106 districts.
2. **Before: decide who is allowed to run.** In Nicaragua in 2021, the election authorities banned three opposition parties before the vote.
3. **After: control the count.** In Istanbul in 2019, the opposition won the election for mayor. The election authorities cancelled the result and ordered a new vote.

Venezuela in 2024 shows how far this can go. The election authorities said that President Nicolás Maduro had won with 51.2 percent. But they never published the results from the polling stations, so nobody could check the count. The Carter Center, an American organization that observes elections around the world, said that the election “cannot be considered democratic”.

#### Good to know
- **Gerrymandering** is the usual name for drawing district borders to help one party.
- **Nicaragua 2021.** The police also arrested seven people who wanted to run for president.
- **Istanbul 2019.** The opposition candidate, Ekrem İmamoğlu, also won the new vote in June 2019, and by many more votes than the first time. So here the plan failed. In March 2025 he was arrested and put in prison.
- **Venezuela after 2024.** The opposition collected copies of the count sheets from most polling stations. They showed a clear win for its candidate, Edmundo González. Maduro stayed in power anyway. In January 2026, US forces captured Maduro. His vice-president, Delcy Rodríguez, has led the country since then. This is not part of your talk, but someone may ask.
- **The photo** shows the building of Venezuela’s election authority in Caracas. Its name is the National Electoral Council, or CNE.

#### If someone asks
**“If the result is decided anyway, why hold an election at all?”**
Because elections make a government look democratic, at home and abroad. That is why backsliding keeps the elections but takes away their fairness.

### Slide 9 · Can it be undone? Hungary 2010 to 2026

#### What it means
This slide tells Hungary’s story from the beginning to today. Then it asks how the damage can be repaired.

- **April 2026.** The opposition party Tisza, led by Péter Magyar, wins 141 of 199 seats. This is also more than two thirds. Fidesz falls to 52 seats after 16 years in power.

The president is close to the old ruling party, and he blocks the new laws. The EU closed its rule-of-law case against Poland in 2024, after the new government promised reforms. #### Good to know
- **What changed in July 2026.** The change ended the president’s term early. It brought back older rules for the Constitutional Court, so four judges had to leave, also the head of the court. And it limits MPs to twelve years in parliament. In June, parliament had already limited prime ministers to eight years, counting past years. So Orbán cannot return as prime minister. In August, parliament elected a new president, András Baka, a former head of the Supreme Court.

- **Poland in detail.** Parliament passed three repair laws in 2024. The president sent them to the Constitutional Tribunal, a court that is close to the old ruling party, and it cancelled all three. In February 2026 the new president, Karol Nawrocki, stopped another court law with his veto. A veto means that he refuses to sign a law. The old ruling party is called Law and Justice, or PiS. The next election for parliament is in 2027.
- **The photo** shows a crowd at a concert in Budapest on 10 April 2026, two days before the election.

**“Is Hungary a democracy again?”**

#### Help for the discussion
**Question 1: If every step is legal, when does a democracy stop being one, and who decides?**

- There is no single moment. That is what makes backsliding hard to see.
- Researchers look at results, not at single laws. Are elections still free and fair? Can courts and journalists still work freely? V-Dem says that Hungary crossed this line in 2018.

**Question 2: Wolin and Tocqueville: is this a misuse of majority rule, or just majority rule?**

- “Just majority rule”: the government won the election. Changing laws is what a majority does.
- “A misuse”: democracy is more than counting votes. It also needs limits, so that the losers of today can win next time. A majority that removes these limits ends the competition.
- Tocqueville warned of a “tyranny of the majority”. Wolin notes that Tocqueville gave no proof of one in America as a whole.

**Question 3: Hungary: is it right to repair the damage with the same fast, two-thirds methods?**

- “Yes”: the old system was built to block change. Without fast action, the election win would change little. And the government promises a new constitution with a public debate.
- “No”: Human Rights Watch says that good goals do not excuse bad methods. And every future majority can copy the method.

## 4. The three readings in simple words

Your talk uses three course readings. This part tells you what each one says and where you use it. It is based on the published summaries of the texts and on the passages that your slides use. It does not replace reading the texts.

### Haggard and Kaufman (2021): “The Anatomy of Democratic Backsliding”

- **Who.** Stephan Haggard (University of California, San Diego) and Robert Kaufman (Rutgers University). The article is in the *Journal of Democracy*, pages 27 to 41.
- **Their question.** How do elected leaders weaken the limits on their power, take away rights and make elections less fair?
- **What they did.** They compared sixteen countries in which this happened between 1974 and 2019.
- **Their answer.** Three things work together. First, **polarization**: a divided society loses trust in its institutions, and parties move to the extremes. Second, **control of parliament**: a leader with a loyal parliament can remove the other limits in a legal way. They call this the “collapse of the separation of powers”. Third, **small steps**: each step is small, so the danger is hard to see and hard to fight, until it is too late.
- **Where you use it.** Slide 2 (the definition), slide 4 (the path and its numbers), slide 5 (parliament) and slide 9 (Hungary in 2010).
- **In one sentence.** Backsliding needs a divided society, a leader who controls parliament, and many small steps.

### Waldner and Lust (2018): “Unwelcome Change: Coming to Terms with Democratic Backsliding”

- **Who.** David Waldner (University of Virginia) and Ellen Lust (University of Gothenburg). The article is in the *Annual Review of Political Science*, pages 93 to 113.
- **What kind of text.** A review article. It does not study new cases. It puts in order what researchers know, and what they do not know yet.
- **Message 1: what backsliding is.** It is a change *inside* a system, not a jump from democracy to dictatorship. The quality of democracy gets worse through “a discontinuous series of incremental actions”: small steps with pauses.
- **Message 2: how to see it.** They look at three things. **Competition**: are elections still a fair race? **Participation**: can all people still take part? **Accountability**: do leaders still have to explain their actions, and can they be punished? They speak of backsliding only when at least two of the three get worse. They also show that different data sets give very different answers.
- **Message 3: how to explain it.** They go through six groups of theories: leaders, culture, institutions, the economy, social groups and coalitions, and international actors. Their result: there is no ready theory that explains backsliding.
- **Where you use it.** Slide 2 (“step by step”).
- **In one sentence.** Backsliding is a slow change inside a system, and researchers do not yet agree on how to measure or explain it.

### Wolin (2001): *Tocqueville between Two Worlds*, chapter XII

- **Who.** Sheldon Wolin (1922 to 2015) was an American political thinker at Princeton University. Chapter XII is called “Majority Rule or Majority Politics”, pages 241 to 259.
- **Who was Tocqueville?** Alexis de Tocqueville was a French writer. He travelled through the United States in 1831 and 1832 and wrote the famous book *Democracy in America*.
- **Tocqueville’s fear.** In a democracy the majority decides. So the majority can become too strong and crush everybody who thinks differently. He called this the “tyranny of the majority”.
- **Two points from Wolin in your talk.** First, a majority holds “the legal means of achieving revolutionary ends without having to resort to revolutionary means”. In simple words: it can change everything without breaking the law. Wolin says that this fear comes from the American and the French revolutions. Second, Wolin notes that Tocqueville gave no proof of a tyranny of the majority in America as a whole.
- **Where you use it.** Slide 4 (the box at the bottom) and slide 10 (question 2).
- **In one sentence.** A majority can change a whole system in a legal way, and that is exactly what backsliding governments do.
- **Note.** This guide covers only the parts of the chapter that your talk uses.

> **How the three readings fit together**
> Waldner and Lust tell you **what** backsliding is and how hard it is to measure. Haggard and Kaufman tell you **how** it happens. Wolin gives you the old question behind it: may a majority do anything it wants?

## 6. Questions your class may ask

**“What does this have to do with migration?”**
This is not on your slides. It is a ready answer in case the question comes. There are two links.

1. **Fear of migration helps backsliding.** Leaders use it to divide society, and to give the state more powers. In September 2015, when many refugees arrived, Hungary’s government declared a “crisis situation caused by mass immigration”. Parliament then gave the police more powers and let the army help at the border. In 2016 the crisis situation was extended to the whole country, and it was renewed again and again for years.
2. **Backsliding makes people leave.** Nearly 7.9 million people have left Venezuela to find safety and a better life, says the UN refugee agency. In Venezuela, people could not change the government by voting, because the referees were captured.

**“Is backsliding only done by right-wing governments?”**
No. The sixteen countries of Haggard and Kaufman include governments of the left, for example in Venezuela, Bolivia, Ecuador and Nicaragua. They also include governments of the right, for example in Hungary, Poland and Turkey.

**“Can it happen in rich, old democracies?”**
Yes. Haggard and Kaufman include the United States in their sixteen countries. And the V-Dem report of 2026 has Italy, the United Kingdom and the United States on its list.

**“Why only three referees? What about the media?”**
The media are a target too. The V-Dem report says that censoring the media is the tool that such governments use most often. Your talk looks at parliament, courts and election authorities because its topic is the capture of state institutions.

**“What can stop backsliding?”**
Your talk shows one answer: elections, as long as they are still fair enough. Voters changed the government in Poland in 2023 and in Hungary in 2026. A second answer is pressure from outside. The EU held back about 17 billion euros from Hungary because of rule-of-law problems.

**“Where do your numbers come from?”**
From the V-Dem Institute, from Milan Svolik and from Haggard and Kaufman. All sources are on slide 11.
